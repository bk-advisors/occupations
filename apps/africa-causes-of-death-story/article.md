# Causes of Death in Africa

**What 8.3 million deaths in 2021 looked like, by age band**

_Matthew Kuch, June 2026. ~1,800 words. Data: WHO Global Health Estimates 2021 (47-country WHO African Region; excludes North African countries assigned to the Eastern Mediterranean Region)._

---

## The latest Ebola outbreak

Over a month ago, on 15 May, another Ebola outbreak was declared in Uganda. The pattern that follows is familiar to anyone who lives here. A confirmed case. A press conference then global media frenzy. An emergency command centre setup at MOH. WHO and Africa CDC teams deployed to support the situation. Its all too familiar. It has happened before, and it will likely happen again.

Since the virus was first identified in 1976, Ebola has cumulatively killed around **15,000 people** across every outbreak in every affected country combined. Each individual death is a tragedy of course. However, the half-century total, summed across DRC, Uganda, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea and the rest, fits inside a single bad week of malaria mortality on the African continent.

That contrast got me thinking.

We organise our attention around emergencies, and our health systems follow the same pattern. Press conferences, donor pledges, special task forces and parliamentary hearings reliably follow outbreaks. They almost never follow the steady, recurring, predictable causes that do most of the killing.

So I wanted to step back and look at the bigger picture. What do Africa's **8.3 million deaths in 2021** look like when you break them down by cause and by age, separately from the news cycle?

_If you would rather poke at every cause yourself, an [explorable version of this dataset](https://bk-advisors.github.io/africa-causes-of-death-explore/) is here. Same numbers, no narrative. This piece is the narrative._

## The big picture

The full chart sits in three groups, drawn from the standard WHO/GBD taxonomy:

- **Communicable, maternal, perinatal and nutritional conditions**, the things we associate with poverty. The biggest bucket on the continent at **4.6 million deaths**, or 56% of the total.
- **Non-communicable diseases**: heart disease, cancer, diabetes, stroke. **2.9 million deaths**, or 35%.
- **Injuries**: road crashes, drowning, violence, self-harm. **770,000 deaths**, or 9%.

There is a fourth thing that does not fit any of those neatly: COVID-19. In 2021, COVID killed around **493,000 people** in the WHO African Region, its first and largest year of measurable impact on the continent so far. In the chart, COVID sits under "Respiratory infections" alongside lower respiratory infections.

The three-bucket split is a useful starting point, but it is a half-truth. A death is a death in aggregate. What this picture does not show is _when_ in a life each death lands. That, it turns out, is most of what matters.

## The first five years

Under-five mortality looks like a horror show.

**Malaria** is the single largest killer of African children: around **450,000 under-five deaths in 2021** from a disease we have known how to prevent for the better part of a century.

**Lower respiratory infections**, mostly pneumonia, killed another **427,000 children** under five. Most of those deaths are preventable with vaccines (pneumococcal, Hib, measles) and basic clinical care.

**Preterm birth complications** killed **381,000 babies**. **Diarrhoeal disease**, which we treat with packets of salt and sugar costing thirty cents, killed **281,000**. **Birth asphyxia and birth trauma** killed another **280,000**.

Five causes, over 1.8 million child deaths in a single year. The cheapest, most-proven interventions in all of public health line up exactly against this list. The shortfall is in delivery, not in knowing what to do.

## The quiet years (5 to 14)

If a child makes it past five, the next decade is statistically the safest stretch of a human life.

Across the WHO African Region, deaths in the 5-to-14 age band fall to about 460,000, less than a fifth of the under-5 toll. What remains is a different mix: **road injuries** (about 36,000 deaths), **drowning** (24,000), and the long tail of communicable diseases that hang on longer than they should (LRI, diarrhoea, malaria, meningitis, each still killing tens of thousands of school-age children).

The shape is worth noting. The diseases of poverty start to fade. What fills in are the accidental hazards of childhood: water, fire, traffic. After malaria, the single biggest mortality threat to a school-age child across most of Africa is something neither a doctor nor a vaccine can fix.

## The productive years (15 to 49)

The picture changes again. By the time someone enters their fifteenth year and starts looking like an adult, the leading causes of death shift entirely. The big four in this age band:

- **HIV/AIDS**: around **245,000 deaths** in 15-to-49 alone, still the single largest killer of working-age Africans three decades into the ARV era.
- **Tuberculosis**: **146,000**.
- **Road injuries**: **105,000**.
- **COVID-19**: **98,000** in this age band in 2021 alone.

Interpersonal violence (assault, homicide) killed another 92,000 people aged 15-49. Self-harm took 49,000. Maternal disorders, by definition concentrated entirely in this age band, killed about **151,000 women**.

These are deaths of people in the middle of working, parenting and contributing economically. The lives lost in this band carry household-level consequences for everyone around them. The maternal-mortality number alone should haunt every African ministry of health. SDG 3.1, the global goal of fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030, is the policy commitment that says these are deaths the world has agreed should not happen. Only four African countries currently meet that bar. The other 43 do not.

## The NCD wave (50 and older)

The older age bands show a different set of causes. The communicable lines mostly recede, and the chart fills with **ischaemic heart disease, stroke, hypertensive heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, kidney disease**.

This is the "transition" public-health people talk about. As populations live longer and eat differently and move less, the diseases that killed Western populations through the second half of the 20th century are starting to dominate here. By the time you reach the 70+ band, ischaemic heart disease (208,000 deaths) and the two strokes (haemorrhagic 114,000, ischaemic 148,000) together are responsible for roughly a third of African deaths.

(Two things worth flagging in passing. First, African stroke is _predominantly haemorrhagic_: 321,000 deaths against 206,000 ischaemic, almost the inverse of the Western pattern, and a known marker of poorly-controlled hypertension. Second, COVID-19 stays in the top five right through the 70+ band, killing more than 200,000 older Africans in 2021. It will fade from the picture over time, but in this snapshot it is impossible to look away from.)

The interventions that work for NCDs (blood-pressure medication, statins, basic cancer screening, dialysis, insulin) are not the same interventions that work for communicable diseases. They are, on the whole, more expensive, more chronic, and require more sustained contact with the health system. The health systems being asked to deliver them are the same ones already overstretched trying to deliver bednets, ORS and antiretrovirals to younger cohorts.

## The double burden

Policy people call this the **double burden of disease**. Africa is, as far as anyone can tell, the only continent currently in the thick of both halves at the same time. We are dying twice. Once young, from things our great-grandparents died of, and once older, from things our grandparents in the West died of. The two waves overlap.

Most of the world's health systems were built for one wave or the other. The European model was designed in the late 20th century for an aged population whose communicable threats had already been defeated. The model the WHO exported to low-income countries in the 1980s and 1990s was designed for a young population whose NCDs were rare enough to ignore.

Africa is being asked to do both at once, quietly, on a fraction of the per-capita budget. What we need is a third model: a system that can deliver a vaccine campaign to a five-year-old and a statin prescription to a sixty-five-year-old in the same week, on the same budget, often through the same nurse.

## The cheap deaths

I want to come back to the contrast at the beginning, because it is the lever I keep pulling on.

The interventions that would prevent most under-5 deaths in Africa are, by global health standards, almost embarrassingly cheap:

- An insecticide-treated bednet costs about **$3** and prevents roughly half a case of malaria per person-year of use.
- A full course of childhood vaccines through the Expanded Programme on Immunization costs around **$40 per child**.
- A packet of oral rehydration salts costs **30 cents**.
- Skilled birth attendance costs maybe **$50 per delivery**, and would prevent a huge fraction of those 381,000 preterm complications, 280,000 birth asphyxia deaths, and 151,000 maternal deaths.

The NCD interventions piling up in the older age bands cost orders of magnitude more per life saved. A year of hypertension management. A breast-cancer screening program. A dialysis chair.

I am not arguing for triage. The point is that the cheapest possible health gains on the continent are still going unrealized at scale, while the system is simultaneously being asked to take on more expensive new ones. The bottleneck is delivery, and underneath that, financing.

## The root, again

I have written before about how I think the root cause of Africa's underinvestment in health is fiscal capacity: how much tax revenue a government can credibly raise from its own economy. I won't rehearse the whole argument here. (If you missed it: tap, buckets, fruit vendor.)

The causes-of-death picture you have just scrolled through is the same argument from a different angle. The cheapest interventions go undelivered because the systems that should deliver them are starved of resources. The expensive interventions go unbought because the budgets that should buy them are already overdrawn. Every death in this article (neonatal, malaria, maternal, HIV, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, road) sits somewhere on that broken supply chain.

When we talk about "the burden of disease in Africa", we are really talking about **the burden of being asked to do everything, at the same time, on budgets designed for nothing**.

## What can we do?

Three things, briefly. The same three I keep coming back to.

1. **Fund the cheap things to completion.** Bednets, vaccines, ORS, skilled birth attendance, antiretrovirals. The unit economics are so good that "not yet at full coverage" is, at this point, a policy choice rather than a technical limitation.

2. **Build delivery capacity that can carry the next wave.** A nurse who can deliver a vaccine and a statin is worth more than two specialists who can each deliver only one. Primary care over tertiary.

3. **Fix the tap.** Domestic fiscal capacity is the constraint underneath all the others. Until African governments can raise enough of their own revenue, predictably and recurrently, every other reform sits on borrowed time.

The next outbreak will come. It will be in the news. The press conferences and emergency operations centres will assemble, as they should. The causes that take far more lives, year after year, will not be in the news. They are on the chart you have just scrolled.

I hope that by the time of the _next_ next outbreak, that chart looks different.

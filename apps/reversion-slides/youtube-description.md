## Title

Pick one. All three are literal rather than clever, which is the house rule and
also what survives a search result.

1. **Why the children of extraordinary people land closer to average**
2. **Regression to the mean, explained with 40 famous families**
3. **The arithmetic that makes dynasties fade**

The first is the most accurate. The second is the most searchable. The third is
the most clickable and the loosest, since "fade" implies decline where the piece
argues the opposite for Curry, Watson and Lauder.

## Description

> Cornelius Vanderbilt died in 1877 the richest man in America. Ninety six
> years later, at the family's first reunion, a descendant counted a hundred and
> twenty relatives and not one millionaire.
>
> The usual explanation is spending. But the same shape turns up in families that
> spent nothing at all: in height, in exam scores, in batting averages, in second
> albums. Something very ordinary is going on, and Francis Galton found it in
> 1886 while trying to prove the opposite.
>
> This is regression to the mean. Not a force, not a punishment, and not a story
> about anyone failing. Just one multiplication, and what it does to families
> when you are far enough from average for it to matter.
>
> Built from Galton's own tally of 928 adult children, published parent-child
> correlations for height, earnings, wealth, schooling, IQ, lifespan and surname
> status, and 40 families from sport, politics and business placed on a single
> scale. Every placement that is a judgement rather than a measurement carries an
> uncertainty band, and the sample's bias gets its own chapter rather than a
> footnote.
>

> SOURCES AND METHOD
> Galton, F. (1886), Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature,
> Journal of the Anthropological Institute 15, 246-263.
> Silventoinen et al. (2003) on height. Chetty, Hendren, Kline & Saez (2014) on
> the rank-rank earnings slope. Charles & Hurst (2003) on wealth. Bouchard &
> McGue (1981) on IQ. Hertz et al. (2007) on schooling. Ruby et al. (2018) on
> lifespan. Clark (2014), The Son Also Rises, on surname status. Career figures
> from Basketball-Reference, Baseball-Reference, Hockey-Reference and
> Pro-Football-Reference. Historical fortunes from Klepper & Gunther, The Wealthy
> 100, and the Forbes lists. Photographs from Wikimedia Commons, credited in full
> at the end of the film.
>
> A note on what this does not claim: it does not predict any individual, and the
> 95% range around a single child covers most of the ordinary adult range. The 40
> families are famous families, chosen because they are famous, which biases the
> line before anyone draws it. That is what the survivor chapter is for.

## Pinned comment

> The forty families in this are a biased sample and I say so in the video, but
> it is worth repeating here: they are on the chart because both generations left
> a number behind. The children who quietly stopped being measurable are not low
> dots, they are absent, and that means every figure here is a floor rather than
> an estimate. Sources and the full method are in the description.
